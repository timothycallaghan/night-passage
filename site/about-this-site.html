About this site

This site was built by a small studio of AI agents I set up on MausBot, with me playing the client. Here’s how it came together, including the bits that went sideways.

01The idea

It started with André Elias’s site: an immersive Three.js scene behind a simple set of personal links. I loved the idea and wanted my own take on it, with a completely different look.

My brief was a rainy neon street at night, with ordinary people getting on with life under flying cars and future tech. Mostly Blade Runner, with a hint of The Fifth Element. If you’re a fan of the synth blues in Vangelis’s Blade Runner score, I’d turn the sound on for this one. I love what Suno did with Neon Rain Blues (Deckard).

I also wanted to trial an end-to-end agentic website production process, with me as the client doing none of the work except review, feedback and approvals. I wanted to collaborate with AI to get there, rather than one-shot an output and work backwards, and to follow a real studio process with gates, checks and balances.

02A web studio staffed by spiders

Parker (as in Peter) handles creative direction. Charlotte (of the web) writes the code. Shelob (Tolkien’s) does independent QA, because she catches bugs. Roland leads delivery. His name was inspired by the 1980s Roland Rat Superstar, which seemed fitting for MausBot. He isn’t a spider, and I did ask him not to eat the spiders.

I’m the client: I make the creative calls and approve anything that goes public. Each agent has its own lane and its own GitHub identity, and nobody signs off their own work. The names stayed the same throughout, while the models behind them changed.

03Gates, and going round again

Before anyone wrote site code, Roland wrote the process. Work passes through eight gates, G0 to G7, and every gate has a named owner who says yes. On paper it’s a neat line. In practice it’s a loop.

It began with a client interview. Roland asked who the site was for, what a visitor should do, how it should feel, which fonts (I’m fussy about fonts), which links mattered, whether it should make a sound and how it should behave on my phone. He forgot to ask where it would live, so I added hosting and the domain myself.

04Getting the city right

The first concept images were flat 2D drawings. Every file was there, every checksum matched, and they looked nothing like a 3D city.

After that it was passes and side-by-side comparisons. Charlotte’s first build put the camera at eye level in a deep street canyon, and that framing survived everything after it. Later passes drifted too close, so we went back. Parker added people, stalls, window life, neon and a billboard. We ported the whole scene to WebGPU as an experiment, compared the two like for like and kept WebGL. The final rounds came from me prodding it on my phone.

05What’s made, what’s generated

Most of the city is code. Buildings, signs, textures, rain and steam are generated in your browser as the page loads. The three people nearest the camera are adapted from Microsoft’s open Rocketbox library. The newsreader on the billboard is fictional: one still image from OpenAI’s gpt-image-2, made to sway, blink and move her mouth with small image warps. There’s no video or lip-sync behind it. The adverts are original animations. I made “Neon Rain Blues (Deckard)” with Suno. It plays alongside six layers of street sound from ElevenLabs. Nothing plays until you turn sound on.

06From branch to street

Every change goes on its own branch and through a pull request, and an agent who didn’t write it reviews that exact version before it reaches the protected main branch. GitHub Actions builds the same static files each time, and Cloudflare Workers serves them on the free plan. The site is just static files, with no analytics script on the page.

While we iterated, builds went to a private preview that only I could open. Now it’s public, right here.

07What I learned

What worked

  • A clear owner for every decision.
  • Independent review before anything counted as done.
  • A concrete reference to aim at, and frozen versions to compare.
  • Specialists iterating while I made the creative calls.

What caught us out

  • Correct files and passing tests didn’t mean it looked right.
  • Mocks passed. The real provider’s API didn’t, the first time.
  • A browser pretending to be a phone isn’t a phone, and a mix nobody has listened to isn’t finished.
  • My own feedback caught motion, layout and sound problems the checks missed.
  • Being asked to sign in to services that were already connected.

Next time I want real-device and listening tests earlier, hosting prepared at the start, a rights register from day one and smaller slices, and to reuse the icons and deployment path from this build.

The city’s still running behind this panel. Head back and have a look around.
